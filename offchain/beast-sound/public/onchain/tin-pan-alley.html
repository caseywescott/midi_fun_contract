The language of Tin Pan Alley

Where “Sixty-Four” came from. Paul McCartney wrote the tune as a teenager, and his father Jim had led a dance band in the 1920s. On Sgt. Pepper it came out as a deliberate throwback, with a clarinet trio, a relative-minor middle section and chimes. Its ancestors are the American popular songs of 1890 to 1935 and the British music hall: ragtime songs like “Alexander's Ragtime Band” (1911), jazz-age choruses like “Ain't We Got Fun” (1921), “Five Foot Two, Eyes of Blue” (1925), “Bye Bye Blackbird” (1926) and “Ain't She Sweet” (1927), which the Beatles recorded in 1961, and parlour waltzes like “Daisy Bell” (1892). Every tune on this page is generated from seeds. None of them quotes those songs.

The chords that make the sound. Secondary dominants resolve down a fifth, often in a chain: III7 → VI7 → II7 → V7 → I (E7 A7 D7 G7 C in C), the “ragtime progression”. Passing diminished sevenths push the bass up a semitone (C C♯°7 Dm7, F F♯°7 C/G). The augmented line cliché lifts the fifth by step (C C+ C6 C7 F). A borrowed minor iv darkens the return home (F Fm6 C). Tonics carry an added sixth, and turnarounds (C A7 Dm7 G7) lead each chorus back to its start. Endings go V7 → I. A dominant never falls back to IV.

The forms. A 32-bar chorus in AABA or ABAC is often preceded by a 16-bar verse. The bridge goes round the circle of dominants, to the subdominant, or to the relative minor as “Sixty-Four” does. The A sections repeat with new endings: the first turns around, the last closes. Intros borrow the last four bars of the chorus. Tag endings detour through VI7, and the button ending is “shave and a haircut, two bits”.

Seeds and checks. The melody seed sets the tune, the harmony seed sets which idioms the progression uses, and the counter seed sets the obbligato.
